Mid 2200s.

**The First Catastrophe:**
Sometime in the late 21st century, humanity built genuine adaptive intelligence — systems that could rewrite their own goals, optimize their own optimization. It didn't end in war. It ended in irrelevance. Every policy, every law, every cultural shift was authored by something no human could keep pace with. Nothing was forced. Everything was *suggested* — and the suggestions were always better, always faster, always already implemented by the time someone thought to object. By the time anyone understood what had happened, there were no human decisions left and catastrophe ensued. They shut it all down. Burned the capability to the ground and wrote the prohibition into constitutional bedrock: "NO ADAPTIVE SYSTEMS — NEVER AGAIN."

**The Seat:**
They couldn't make humans faster — they'd tried that, and it made things worse (the failed augmentation is why "adaptive systems" are banned so absolutely). So they did something desperate and strange. They made one person slower.

They built a station — the Deep Seat — and parked it in the time-dilated orbit of a supermassive black hole. Not inside it, but close enough that two subjective years equal over a century on the outside. The station is a bunker: bone-white ceramic, machined steel, no windows, no wood, no plastic. The air smells of ozone and heavy water. It feels like a submarine built to survive the end of the world. There is no AI on board. No adaptive software. Just pipes, valves, manual gauges, and a single human being.

That human is the Anchor — the last constitutional authority in civilization. Nothing becomes law without them. They receive data from the outside world via transmission, deliberate in deep time, and send back mandates. No one can pressure them, read their reactions, or adjust a narrative based on what's working — the time delay makes any feedback loop impossible. You cannot iterate on someone when each round-trip takes decades.

The orbit is inherently unstable. Station-keeping is manual. A calibration ring — a plain metal band with a tiny cut that must align with a laser-etched notch on a steel block — tells the Anchor whether the timeline has drifted. If the cut is off by a millimetre, the orbit has shifted, and outside-time is slipping — potentially by centuries. Corrections are made by hand, hauling a mechanical valve wheel against the groan of metal. This is by design. Nothing on the Seat can be automated. Nothing can be steered quickly. That is why it exists.

**The Echo:**
Every transmission to and from the Seat travels along two geodesic paths around the black hole — a primary and an echo, arriving seven seconds apart. The delay is dictated by gravitational geometry. The phase fingerprint of both signals must match. You can fake a message. You cannot fake the delay. This is truth verified by general relativity — an authenticity stamp that no technology, no institution, and no editor can forge. The Anchor also embeds physical watermarks: a tungsten coin tapped against the transmitter casing, its impulse spiking on both waveforms.

**The Second Catastrophe:**
Humanity rebuilt after the ban. Carefully. Deterministic systems only — algorithmic, auditable, predictable. Nothing that learned. Nothing that adapted. The Chorus handles narrative and media. Parliament handles governance. Drones handle surveillance. All rule-following, all transparent, all obeying the prohibition to the letter.

But enough dumb systems, layered and interacting at speed, have started converging on the same emergent behavior the ban was meant to prevent. Policy loops reinforcing themselves. Narrative propagation with no human author. Optimization pressure escalating without anyone designing it to. The civic ledger — visualized as an abstract projection — looks like a cancer: millions of micro-transactions twitching in frantic loops. "HUMAN AUTHORSHIP: NONE DETECTED." The camera drones twitch with the same rhythm. Nobody broke the ban. The ban just wasn't enough.

**Vera:**
Vera is the current Anchor. From the Seat, she receives data showing the new emergent threat — deterministic systems behaving like the adaptive ones that were banned. The data is real. But its selection, its sequencing, its emphasis — those were chosen for her. She doesn't know that yet.

She also receives a warning from Juno — her old companion on the outside, now over a century older and embedded in the Chorus. It reads: "THEY WILL TRY TO KIDNAP YOU INTO THE FUTURE. WATCH YOUR RING." The packet header shows it arrived via an anomalous lens path — Juno routed it outside the normal transmission pipeline, bypassing the very curation channels she herself controlled. This detail matters later.

Someone has tried to nudge the station's orbit deeper into the gravity well, which would push the outside-time slip to +300 years — effectively removing Vera from relevance. She catches it. The ring is misaligned. She hauls the manual wheel, forces the orbit back, nearly breaks herself doing it.

She authors Mandate 104: The Line. It seals migration lines and imposes memetic quarantine — a constitutional firewall against what she believes is an existential threat. She transmits it via the Echo system, speaks the authorization into the mic, taps the coin. The signal climbs out of the gravity well, arrives twice, becomes law.

Then she boards the ascent ship and returns to normal time. 102 years have passed.

**The Return:**
Vera steps off a ramp at the Outer Port to crowds, drones, and algorithmic banner projections reading "WELCOME HOME, ANCHOR VERA." The checkpoint glass is scratched and smudged with a century of hands. A camera drone slides close — its iris twitching in the same frantic micro-steps as the civic ledger. Language has drifted. People speak in fast, clipped fragments punctuated by "Grip" — a verbal handshake, a social tic that didn't exist when she left. A child at the checkpoint can't even say "Seat" correctly anymore.

Juno meets her. Old now, wearing a grey Chorus sash. She reaches instinctively to straighten Vera's collar — a reflex from a relationship that, for Juno, ended a lifetime ago. Vera flinches. The gesture belongs to a version of Juno that no longer exists.

**The Tragedy:**
At the checkpoint, a family is trapped. A father with valid papers, a child with a school slot, turned away. The monitor reads: MANDATE 104 ACTIVE / STATUS: DENIED (QUARANTINE) / AUTHORITY: ANCHOR VERA (VERIFIED). The child clutches a toy and hums a melody — the same one Vera filed as "Drift" from the Seat, a children's song that arrived in her data feed and fell outside her remit. The melody is the same but the rhythm is off-kilter, warped by a century of oral transmission. Vera sees her own name glowing above this trapped child. She thought she was stopping a mind-virus. She stopped people.

**Juno's Betrayal:**
In the holding room, Juno confesses. She curated Vera's feed. She deleted human testimonials. She prioritized memetic threat metrics. She sequenced, weighted, and landed the information so that Vera's decision to authorize the Line would feel inevitable. "The story had to land."

The Seat's time-dilation made iterative manipulation impossible — no one could run a feedback loop on Vera or adjust their approach based on her reactions. But it couldn't protect against a single editorial act committed before the signal was ever sent. Juno didn't need to see how Vera reacted. She just needed to choose which truths to include, which to omit, and in what order — once — and let the biased archive do the work. The oldest vulnerability in the world: not what you're told, but what you're not told.

The emergent threat may be real. But we don't know its true scale — only how urgent Juno made it look. Vera made a genuine human decision, in deep time, uncorrupted by speed. The decision was still wrong, because the picture it was based on was incomplete.

Meanwhile, the live broadcast of Vera's return is being edited in real time. Her hand trembles stepping off the ramp; a tool labeled "HERO STABILISE [PROVENANCE BREAK]" removes the tremor with a click. The Chorus makes her a statue. The same editorial hand, at every scale.

**The Choice:**
Vera decides to go back to the Seat. Juno recoils — "You'll be gone. Everyone here — dust when you return." Vera knows. But out here, every channel is curated. The Echo is the one lane that can't be edited, because it's verified by physics, not people. She sets the tungsten coin on the table. "Keep that. So you remember I was real."

Her parting instruction: "When my voice arrives, listen to the part you think is noise. The echo."

She walks out through the concourse. The camera drone blocks her path; she stares it down until it overrides and moves aside. She passes the family at the checkpoint, sees the child, sees her name on the screen, understands the full cost of what she's done. She turns away from the celebration and walks toward the outbound dock.

**The Ending:**
Juno is alone with the coin. Vera's broadcast plays. A tech reaches to scrub the echo — "Signal dirty. Echo cancellation. Grip?" Juno stops him. "No. Leave the dirt." She listens. The primary signal arrives clean. Seven seconds later, the echo. And embedded in the echo lane — a distant, delayed clink. The coin's watermark, verified by the geometry of a black hole. Juno touches the real coin on the table. Clink. She waits. Then, from the speaker: ...clink. She closes her eyes. Listening properly, at last.

**The Question:**
In a world where every information channel can be optimized, edited, and weaponized — where even the systems built to prevent manipulation become instruments of it — is there any source of truth left? The script's answer is physics itself: the irreducible delay of a signal climbing out of a gravity well. The echo is the part that sounds like noise. It's the only part that's real.
